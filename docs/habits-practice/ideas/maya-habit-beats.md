# Maya's habit story: beats for Lesson 2

Section: Habits and daily practice › Maya's habit story
Status: Draft, not approved. Created: 2026-10-09. Updated: 2026-10-09.

This is a first draft of the narrator's words for Maya's chapter in Lesson 2, in the order accepted in [015, structure C](../../product/015-lesson-two-proposal.md#c-a-short-maya-in-lesson-1-her-habit-story-in-lesson-2). It follows the [Wisdom voice](../../../.claude/skills/wisdom-voice/SKILL.md). Nothing here is in the site. Once the owner approves the words, they go into the four catalogs (`src/i18n/messages/<locale>.json`) together.

Lesson 1 keeps three Maya beats: two paths and a slow start; learning builds, with basketball optional; never too late. This chapter doesn't repeat them. It follows the Maya who practises and asks how she keeps getting started. Skill growth stays in Lesson 1.

Under each beat, a line tags its claims, as 015's game table does: **evidence** (with the [habits sources](../../research/habits/sources.md)), **editorial** (our adaptation, not tested) or **fiction** (the story).

## The beats

### 1. Remember Maya?

> This is Maya. She's eight, and she plays on a soccer team. You may have met her in the last lesson, on two imagined paths. On one, she practised most days, and her skills grew. This lesson follows that Maya. But we skipped one question. How did she keep getting started, day after day?

**Fiction:** Maya, her age and her team, from Lesson 1. **Editorial:** the recap names no Path A or B, so the lesson stands alone; "getting started" is treated as its own question, apart from skill ([004](../../product/004-habits-and-daily-practice.md#core-distinction)).

### 2. Wanting is the easy part.

> Team practice starts itself. The coach picks the time, and someone drives Maya there. Practising at home is up to her. Each morning she thinks, "Today, after school." Then there's a snack, a show, a friend at the door. Suddenly it's dinnertime. Wanting to practise is easy. Starting is the tricky part. "Later" is a sneaky time. It never quite arrives.

**Fiction:** her afternoons. **Editorial:** wanting versus starting ([004](../../product/004-habits-and-daily-practice.md#proposed-guided-explanation), step 1), and the contrast between practice someone else schedules and practice you start yourself (015's game). The gap between meaning to and doing is not in the reviewed sources.

### 3. A time, a place and a ball by the door.

> So Maya makes a plan that fits in one sentence. "When I get home from school, I kick my ball against the wall." Then she leaves her ball by the front door. It's hard to ignore a ball in your way. That's the point. Getting home is now Maya's cue. A cue is a reminder to start.

**Evidence**, children: a short if-then plan ("When I get home, I…") helps children reach goals on average, more in younger children; the outcome was reaching the goal, not forming a habit ([B10]). **Evidence**, adults: a consistent time and place goes with stronger habits, but the links are small ([B2]), and a steady study setting helped university students start ([B4]). **Editorial:** the ball by the door as a set-up ([004](../../product/004-habits-and-daily-practice.md#proposed-guided-explanation), step 3); putting things nearer or farther was tested only with food, mostly in adults, at low certainty ([B12]). **Fiction:** the scene.

### 4. The habit gets her to the wall.

> At first, every afternoon takes a push. Slowly, starting gets easier. One day her feet head for the wall on their own. That's a habit: something you start without much thinking. It comes faster for some people than others. The kicks still take thought: aim, miss, try again. The habit gets her to the wall. It doesn't kick for her.

**Evidence**, adults: starting can become more automatic in a steady setting, and that is separate from doing the hard part well ([B4], university students); how fast it happens varies widely between people, with no single timetable ([B1], [B3]; [B5] found 18 to 254 days to level off). **Editorial:** "something you start without much thinking" as the child's word for automaticity ([004](../../product/004-habits-and-daily-practice.md#core-distinction)). **Fiction:** "her feet head for the wall on their own". Untested in children.

### 5. The same trick, for homework.

> Homework is harder to love than a soccer ball. So Maya tries the same trick. After her snack, she opens her notebook at the kitchen table, pencil ready. That's the whole first step: open it. Some sums are tricky. Her mum is nearby, and asking is part of the plan. If her mum's busy, Maya circles the sum for her teacher tomorrow.

**Fiction:** the scene and her mum. **Editorial:** a small first step and a named helper, with a second helper when the first isn't free ([004](../../product/004-habits-and-daily-practice.md#proposed-guided-explanation), step 3). **Evidence**, children: if-then plans ([B10]); training parents raised homework completion and cut homework problems, with achievement gains only "possible" ([B14]); support that respects the child's say goes with better motivation and persistence, and control with less, as associations only ([B15], [B16]). A finished page is not the same as understanding ([findings](../../research/habits/README.md#repeated-choices-and-the-next-choice)).

### 6. A week off, and back again.

> Then Maya catches a cold. A whole week: no wall, no notebook, lots of soup. On Monday, the ball looks a bit lonely. Starting takes a bigger push, and her first kicks feel stiff. But her skills haven't vanished. She doesn't need to catch up. She just starts at the next chance. And a teammate comes round to join her.

**Fiction:** the cold (bad luck) and the teammate (good luck), keeping both kinds of outside force visible. **Evidence**, adults: one missed day did not materially affect forming a habit ([B5]); a week is longer than that, and over longer gaps automaticity weakens gradually, not all at once ([B6]). **Evidence**, school-age pupils: after a whole summer, test scores dipped a little, not to zero ([B9]; tests, not home practice). **Editorial:** "starts at the next chance", no catching up, no streak. Restarting is untested in children ([findings](../../research/habits/README.md#interruptions-and-restarting)).

### 7. When a plan stops working.

> Then her little brother finds a cartoon about singing trucks. He watches it in the kitchen, right at homework time. Maya reads the same sum four times. She still starts on time. She just doesn't get anywhere. So Maya and her mum try a new spot, away from the trucks. Nothing was wrong with Maya. Something was wrong with the kitchen.

**Fiction:** the brother and the trucks. **Editorial:** checking whether a routine still helps and changing one part of it ([004](../../product/004-habits-and-daily-practice.md#proposed-guided-explanation), steps 5 and 6); starting on time is not the same as getting the work done ([B4]). **Evidence**, adults: changing the setting changed food choices, at low certainty ([B12]); a plan for a new response worked only alongside a goal people cared about ([B11], lab tasks); a new setting loosens old cue-linked habits ([B7]). **Evidence**, children, association only: violin pupils enjoyed practice more when parents asked their view and set up a structured place ([B17]). Changing children's routines is untested.

### 8. Your turn. *(The game goes here.)*

> Your turn: fourteen days of soccer, cello and reading. Pick your moments, get things ready, and see what helps you start.

**Fiction:** the game's days. The mechanics and their basis are in [015's game table](../../product/015-lesson-two-proposal.md#a-longer-game-two-weeks-one-routine). The line stays neutral on whether the reader picks one routine or juggles all three, which the game design still has to settle.

### 9. Make your own plan.

> Now you try, on paper. Pick something you'd really like to do more often. Then answer:
>
> - What's one small first step?
> - When and where? "After ___, I'll ___."
> - What can I get ready?
> - Who could help?
> - If I miss a day, when's my next chance?
>
> In a week or two, take a look. Is it helping? Keep your plan, or change it.

**Editorial:** the questions adapt [004's paper plan](../../product/004-habits-and-daily-practice.md#a-small-practical-experiment): no app, account or streak, and the look after a week or two is a check on the plan, not a deadline for a habit. **Evidence**, children: if-then plans help children reach goals ([B10]). **Evidence**, adults: a new plan works only alongside a goal people care about ([B11]), so the reader picks something they want.

### 10. What to take with you.

> - **A time and a place can make starting easier.** Get one thing ready too. Doing it well still takes thought.
> - **A missed week isn't the end.** What you learned doesn't vanish. Start again at the next chance.
> - **If a plan isn't working, change the plan.** Try a new time or place. The plan needs fixing, not you.

**Evidence and editorial**, as in the beats above: the first from [B2] and [B4] (adults) and [B10] (children, goals); the second from [B5] (adults, one missed day) and [B9] (school-age, a summer), with the restart wording editorial; the third editorial ([004](../../product/004-habits-and-daily-practice.md#acceptance-criteria)), drawing on [B11] and [B12] (adults).

## Notes for grown-ups

- **Maya is a made-up story.** We found no study that tested how habits form in primary-school children. Most findings here come from adults. The child evidence is next door: if-then plans, family help with homework and practice, and bedtime routines ([findings](../../research/habits/README.md#children-and-family-support)).
- **Plans, not habits, in children.** In 42 studies, short if-then plans helped children reach goals (g = 0.31, larger in younger children, results varied a lot). Those studies measured reaching a goal, not whether starting became automatic ([B10]).
- **Starting is not the same as doing well.** A routine can help someone begin. It doesn't do the thinking, and it isn't a general habit of good judgment or of willpower. A finished worksheet isn't proof of understanding ([B4]; [findings](../../research/habits/README.md#repeated-choices-and-the-next-choice)).
- **There's no magic number of days.** In one adult study, a new habit took 18 to 254 days to level off, depending on the person; a review found wide differences too. Neither "21 days" nor "66 days" holds as a rule ([B1], [B5]).
- **Missing a day isn't a reset.** In adults, one missed day didn't materially matter ([B5]). Longer breaks can cost a little: habits weaken gradually ([B6]), and after a summer, school test scores dip slightly ([B9]). How children best restart is untested, so the restart wording here is ours. The lesson has no streaks on purpose.
- **Help works best with the child's say.** Structured help raised homework completion ([B14]). Asking a child's view and agreeing practice together go with more enjoyment and motivation; control goes with less persistence. These are associations ([B15], [B16], [B17]).
- **Not everyone has a quiet spot, a wall or a grown-up free at four o'clock.** Maya's fixes are examples. A plan that doesn't fit a child's home is a plan to change, not a failure. Rest and free play are not lost practice.

## Open questions for the owner

1. **Path labels.** Beat 1 mentions "two imagined paths" but never says "Path A". Is that enough for readers coming from Lesson 1, and clear for those who aren't?
2. **Maya's age and family.** She is eight again, in her first season, before her skills grew in Lesson 1. Her mum and a little brother are new characters. Keep them, or use "a grown-up"?
3. **The missed week.** A cold makes it pure bad luck. Should a missed day also come from her own choice (a friend's party, a lazy afternoon), so readers see that a chosen break isn't the end either?
4. **The routine that stops working.** The draft moves homework away from a noisy kitchen. The other option is soccer in winter, when it's dark after school and she moves her kicking to weekend mornings. That one echoes the game's weekends. Which do you prefer?
5. **Reading.** The game has soccer, cello and reading, but Maya's story has soccer and homework. Should a bedtime-reading moment join her story, or is the game enough?
6. **The plan.** A printable card, or only the questions on the page ([004](../../product/004-habits-and-daily-practice.md#open-product-choices))?
