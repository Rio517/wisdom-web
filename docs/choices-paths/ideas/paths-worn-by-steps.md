## Why look at the framing again

The map has changed. Its branches now separate gradually instead of at sharp corners. After Today, the possible paths occupy a narrower band, especially for an older example person. A few paths end. Others move far above or below the main group and stay there.

The copy still talks about single choices: "Some choices open opportunities. Some make the next step harder." Beat 2 of the lesson is built around one choice. The map, however, shows routes separating over time. It does not clearly show a single moment of decision. The words and picture may therefore be teaching different versions of the idea.

## The claim to teach

This is an editorial proposal, not a research finding. Some choices have effects that build over time. What a person learns from one experience can help with the next. Practical preparation can make a later opportunity easier to take. Repeating one useful action, such as checking a bag before leaving, may eventually make that action require less thought.

These are three related but different mechanisms: learning, preparation and a behavior-specific routine. None of them means that all good decisions become automatic, or that effort controls the result. Circumstances, decisions made by other people, support and chance also affect what happens.

The [habits research](../../research/habits/README.md) provides an initial account of habit formation, but not a completed review of how children change established habits. The lesson should therefore make a limited claim: repeating a specific action may help it become more routine. It should not promise a timetable, extend one routine into a general habit of good judgment, or imply that repetition explains every change. The [learning findings](../../research/learning/README.md) address how earlier understanding can help with later learning; this is a different mechanism.

## Useful terms

| Term | What it means in the lesson | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| learning | understanding gained from an earlier experience that can help later | What transfers depends on what was learned and what the next situation requires. |
| preparation | practical work done before an opportunity or difficulty arrives | It can change what is possible without controlling the result. |
| routine | a specific repeated action that may begin to require less thought | One routine is not a general habit of making good choices. |
| opportunity | something a person may be able to do from their present position | Skills, preparation, support, circumstances and chance can all matter. |
| support | useful help from another person | Asking for or receiving help is part of the example, not evidence of weakness. |
| changing route | responding to new information or circumstances | A different destination or a return can be sensible; the summit is not the only success. |

The main connection is: "What happened before can change what is possible next." This is not a claim that earlier choices determine the future.

## Proposed illustration: a series of hikes

This is the sole leading analogy in this proposal. It is an editorial suggestion, not approved lesson copy or a research finding. Many children may be able to picture a hike even if they have not been on one, but that cannot be assumed. The example needs reader testing.

A series of hikes can show effects carrying forward. On an early hike, the group learns what the day demands. Before a later hike, that knowledge can improve their preparation. Repeatedly checking what they packed may become a useful routine. Within each hike, many steps add up, rest and food help the group continue, and weather or trail conditions can change the available routes. Choosing another route or turning back can be a good response, so the summit must not stand for success, worth or the only acceptable ending.

Earlier child-facing candidate, retained as proposal history rather than current lesson copy (about 160 words):

> Noor goes on an all-day mountain hike with two friends and an adult. On the first trip, Noor forgets sunblock and wears shoes that soon feel uncomfortable. The adult has spare sunblock, and the group takes sensible rests and chooses a shorter trail. They enjoy lunch beside a stream.
>
> Before the next hike, Noor remembers what happened. The group checks the route and packs water, snacks, hats and sunblock. Noor chooses more comfortable shoes. No single step carries them very far, but many steps bring them to the waterfall they planned to visit. Rest and food help them keep going through the day.
>
> Over several hikes, Noor starts checking the bag without a reminder. Some days the group goes farther and reaches the place it planned to visit. On another day, bad weather makes them change route or turn back. Preparation and experience change what is practical, but they do not control the weather. Each hike gives the group something to use next time.

The series links the mechanisms without merging them. Remembering an earlier hike is learning. Packing useful items is practical preparation. Checking the bag repeatedly may form one specific routine. The many steps taken during a hike show cumulative action, but they are not themselves evidence that knowledge compounds or that the group has developed a general habit of making good choices.

An earlier paddling analogy remains only a possible backup if reader testing shows that the hiking example is unclear. It is not part of the current recommendation. Any fictional example must remain separate from the private conversations that prompted it.

Draft opening copy, to test with readers (about 45 words):

> **Your future has more than one path.**
> What happens today can change what is possible later. You may learn something, prepare differently or make one useful action part of a routine. Other people, circumstances and chance matter too. Let's follow a fictional group across several hikes.

## A question raised by the map: what does height mean?

Product 001 says vertical position does not represent success or human worth. The map description says the same. Yet some routes in the current drawing move far up or down and stay there. Readers may interpret that as a life going well or badly, even if the text says otherwise.

Two honest options:

1. Keep height without a fixed meaning and say so clearly. Describe the distant routes without calling them better or worse. Put favorable and unfavorable consequences into the worked example, where the lesson can name a specific effect without ranking a person's life.
2. Let height mean how things are going. The picture becomes easier to read and carries more feeling. The cost is a vertical ranking of lives, which the guide has refused so far, for good reasons.

This proposal recommends option 1, with specific consequences explained in the fictional example. The owner has not decided this question. Either option would require later changes to `app.js` and product 001; this idea document does not make those changes.

## Not in this proposal

This lesson is about the impact of choices and how their effects can add up. It does not explain how the mind makes a decision or provide a decision-making checklist. Habits gets its own lesson next; “How we make choices” follows it. The [lesson roadmap](../../README.md#lesson-roadmap) holds the later research leads, including principles, convenience, *The Righteous Mind*, design thinking and constraints. This proposal does not research or validate those ideas.

A quiz, a simulator, relationships and "what makes a happy life" remain outside this proposal. Nothing above should be presented as a new research finding. Any later lesson copy based on "The claim to teach" must keep its limits and distinguish editorial framing from evidence.
