### L1 — Skill accumulation

Flavio Cunha, James J. Heckman, Susanne M. Schennach. *Estimating the Technology of Cognitive and Noncognitive Skill Formation*. Econometrica 78(3), May 2010, 883–931. [Published paper](https://hceconomics.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/pdf/events/Cunha_Heckman_etal_2010_Econometrica_v78_n3.pdf).

Supports: earlier skills can complement later investment. Structural models use longitudinal data on 2,207 firstborn white children in the Children of the NLSY/79. Findings depend on modeling assumptions and concern parental inputs and environments. The model explicitly excludes adult investment. Confidence: moderate for the mechanism; insufficient for individual forecasts or irreversible deadlines.

### L11 — Prior knowledge and new learning

National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine. *How People Learn II: Learners, Contexts, and Cultures*, chapter 5, 2018. [Official chapter](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/24783/chapter/7).

Supports: relevant prior knowledge can facilitate new learning; inappropriate prior knowledge can interfere. Confidence: strong as a broad consensus synthesis, not a quantified lifetime compounding rule. Chapter and summary inspected. The proposed fractions example is authored, not an evaluated lesson from this source.

### L12 — Transfer between sports

Luca Oppici, Derek Panchuk. *Specific and general transfer of perceptual-motor skills and learning between sports: A systematic review*. Psychology of Sport and Exercise 59, 2022, 102118. [Publisher abstract and preview](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1469029221002363).

Supports: transfer can occur, but long-term learning claims are weak. Seventeen low-to-moderate-quality studies, predominantly short-term performance tests; only two examined subsequent learning, with conflicting results. Confidence: moderate for caution about generalization; insufficient for a universal tennis-to-racket-sports advantage. Abstract and publisher excerpts inspected, not complete study tables. The tennis example remains a qualified illustration.

### L13 — Mathematics enjoyment and achievement

Reinhard Pekrun, Stephanie Lichtenfeld, Herbert W. Marsh, Kou Murayama, Thomas Goetz. *Achievement Emotions and Academic Performance: Longitudinal Models of Reciprocal Effects*. Child Development 88(5), 2017, 1653–1670. [Author-hosted paper](https://motivationsciencelab.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/Kou-et-al._2017.pdf).

Supports: positive emotions and maths achievement predicted each other over five annual waves among 3,425 German students in grades 5–9, mean starting age 11.7. Confidence: moderate for the longitudinal association, not an individually guaranteed or experimentally established loop. Abstract, design, and causal qualifications inspected in the paper. Self-selected practice is an illustrative mechanism, not the intervention tested.
