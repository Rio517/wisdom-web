## Frameworks

<a id="c1"></a>
### C1 — Elder 1998 — life-course theory

Glen H. Elder Jr. *The Life Course as Developmental Theory*. Child Development 69(1), February 1998, 1–12. DOI: [10.1111/j.1467-8624.1998.tb06128.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.1998.tb06128.x). [Full-text copy](https://scispace.com/pdf/the-life-course-as-developmental-theory-96a1hzmecu.pdf).

- **Population/design:** Theoretical synthesis grounded chiefly in longitudinal US studies of Great Depression cohorts; not a single new sample or experiment.
- **Measured outcome:** Not applicable. It develops principles of historical time/place, timing, linked lives and agency within opportunities and constraints.
- **Confidence:** High that these are canonical organizing principles; not an effect estimate.
- **Limits/access:** Full text inspected. Historical cohort examples do not show that one event fixes a later outcome or that the framework predicts an individual life.

<a id="c2"></a>
### C2 — Dannefer 2003 — cumulative advantage and disadvantage

Dale Dannefer. *Cumulative Advantage/Disadvantage and the Life Course: Cross-Fertilizing Age and Social Science Theory*. The Journals of Gerontology: Series B 58(6), November 2003, S327–S337. DOI: [10.1093/geronb/58.6.S327](https://doi.org/10.1093/geronb/58.6.S327). [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14614120/), [full-text scan](https://artscimedia.case.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/118/2014/11/15005927/CumulativeAdvantageDisadvantageandtheLifeCourse.pdf).

- **Population/design:** Conceptual review of sociological and life-course literatures.
- **Measured outcome:** Not applicable. It distinguishes processes through which relative advantage/disadvantage may accumulate through institutions and time.
- **Confidence:** High for the conceptual warning that mechanisms and social organization matter; no universal widening rate follows.
- **Limits/access:** Full text inspected. “Cumulative disadvantage” has multiple uses; the paper does not establish that every gap grows or every early condition persists.

<a id="c3"></a>
### C3 — Mitchell, Levin and Krumboltz 1999 — planned happenstance

Kathleen E. Mitchell, Al S. Levin and John D. Krumboltz. *Planned Happenstance: Constructing Unexpected Career Opportunities*. Journal of Counseling & Development 77(2), April 1999, 115–124. DOI: [10.1002/j.1556-6676.1999.tb02431.x](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1556-6676.1999.tb02431.x). [Full-text copy](https://www.vkotocka.si/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/Mitchell-K.E.-idr.-%C4%8Clanek.-Planned-happenstance-constructing-unexpected-career-opportunities.pdf).

- **Population/design:** Counseling theory illustrated with practice cases; not a controlled trial.
- **Measured outcome:** Not applicable. It proposes exploration plus curiosity, persistence, flexibility, optimism and risk-taking as ways to notice and act on unplanned opportunities.
- **Confidence:** Moderate as a useful counseling framework; low for causal claims about its prescribed skills.
- **Limits/access:** Full text inspected. Exploration can change exposure or readiness, not control chance; using this theory to blame someone for missed luck would reverse its purpose.

<a id="c4"></a>
### C4 — Rice 2014 — chance in career theory and research

Alexander Rice. *Incorporation of Chance Into Career Development Theory and Research*. Journal of Career Development 41(5), October 2014, 445–463; first online October 16, 2013. DOI: [10.1177/0894845313507750](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894845313507750). [Publisher record and abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0894845313507750).

- **Population/design:** Narrative review of career-development theory and research.
- **Measured outcome:** Reviews how theories treat events that are unpredictable and unplanned for the person experiencing them.
- **Confidence:** Moderate for the review's diagnosis that direct empirical attention to chance was limited; not evidence that a particular intervention works.
- **Limits/access:** Abstract, publisher metadata and references inspected; full article was paywalled. It concerns careers, not childhood development generally.

<a id="c5"></a>
### C5 — Sen 2005 — capability approach

Amartya Sen. *Human Rights and Capabilities*. Journal of Human Development 6(2), July 2005, 151–166. DOI: [10.1080/14649880500120491](https://doi.org/10.1080/14649880500120491). [Publisher full text](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14649880500120491), [archival PDF](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=311a84649880fb6eb22f842a46b1f98bee415a2a&repid=rep1&type=pdf).

- **Population/design:** Normative and philosophical analysis; no participant sample.
- **Measured outcome:** Not applicable. It distinguishes capabilities—substantive freedoms or real opportunities—from resources and achieved functionings.
- **Confidence:** High for the framework's own distinctions; it does not provide a causal model or an agreed exhaustive capability list.
- **Limits/access:** Full text inspected. The approach helps ask what someone can actually do with resources; it does not determine a person's worth, forecast their future or replace attention to processes and rights.

<a id="c6"></a>
### C6 — Domínguez-Serrano and del Moral-Espín 2022 — capability approach and children

Mónica Domínguez-Serrano and Lucía del Moral-Espín. *The Capability Approach and Child Well-Being: A Systematic Literature Review*. Child Indicators Research 15(6), December 2022, 2043–2063; online July 23, 2022. DOI: [10.1007/s12187-022-09953-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12187-022-09953-1). [Repository record](https://rodin.uca.es/handle/10498/27458), [full text](https://d-nb.info/1269571427/34).

- **Population/design:** Systematic review of 144 documents from 2000–2020 found through Web of Science, Scopus and the Human Development and Capability Association; 37 specifically addressed child well-being.
- **Measured outcome:** Mapped methods, regions and uses of capability concepts in child-well-being research.
- **Confidence:** High for the review's map of its searched literature; lower for any substantive child-outcome generalization because applications were diverse.
- **Limits/access:** Full text inspected. Europe was overrepresented, cross-country work was scarce and capability concepts were used inconsistently. The review supports treating children as present agents, not a universal capability checklist.

<a id="c7"></a>
### C7 — Bronfenbrenner 1977 — ecological development

Urie Bronfenbrenner. *Toward an Experimental Ecology of Human Development*. American Psychologist 32(7), July 1977, 513–531. DOI: [10.1037/0003-066X.32.7.513](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.32.7.513). [Full-text copy](https://library.scottbarrykaufman.com/uploads/2019/07/a4f4a4545519d84c5c57095b4bcc685d7dd9.pdf).

- **Population/design:** Theoretical and methodological article using illustrative developmental studies.
- **Measured outcome:** Not a new outcome study. It describes reciprocal development within immediate settings and their interconnections, external settings and larger social patterns.
- **Confidence:** High for the foundational framework; empirical claims must come from particular studies.
- **Limits/access:** Full text inspected. Bronfenbrenner's theory later developed; a static set of nested circles is an incomplete account of person, process, context and time.

<a id="c8"></a>
### C8 — Tudge et al. 2009 — uses and misuses of bioecological theory

Jonathan R. H. Tudge, Irina Mokrova, Bridget E. Hatfield and Rachana B. Karnik. *Uses and Misuses of Bronfenbrenner's Bioecological Theory of Human Development*. Journal of Family Theory & Review 1(4), December 2009, 198–210. DOI: [10.1111/j.1756-2589.2009.00026.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1756-2589.2009.00026.x). [Full-text copy](https://scispace.com/pdf/uses-and-misuses-of-bronfenbrenner-s-bioecological-theory-of-1exy5zuu46.pdf).

- **Population/design:** Theory-use audit of 25 empirical family/development articles published since 2001 that cited Bronfenbrenner.
- **Measured outcome:** Whether studies used the mature theory's process, person, context and time components; the authors judged only four to use it appropriately.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-to-high for this bounded audit, not a systematic estimate of developmental effects.
- **Limits/access:** Full text inspected. Selection and coding reflect the authors' interpretation; the paper warns against using “ecological” as a loose synonym for context.

## External setbacks and recovery

<a id="c9"></a>
### C9 — Oreopoulos, Page and Stevens 2008 — intergenerational worker displacement

Philip Oreopoulos, Marianne Page and Ann Huff Stevens. *The Intergenerational Effects of Worker Displacement*. Journal of Labor Economics 26(3), July 2008, 455–483. DOI: [10.1086/588493](https://doi.org/10.1086/588493). [Author manuscript](https://poverty.ucdavis.edu/sites/main/files/file-attachments/stevens_2008jole_finalman.pdf).

- **Population/design:** Canadian administrative records for more than 39,000 father–son pairs, 1978–1999; quasi-experimental comparisons around fathers' firm closures. The published paper's main sample describes sons ages 10–14 around the 1980–1982 displacement window.
- **Measured outcome:** Sons' adult earnings and receipt of unemployment insurance/social assistance. Average annual earnings were about 9% lower, with effects concentrated in the lowest family-income quartile.
- **Confidence:** Moderate causal confidence locally, conditional on firm-closure and comparison assumptions.
- **Limits/access:** Full text inspected. Sons only, historical Canada, and local shocks/selection around closures constrain generalization. An average association is not a child's destiny.

<a id="c10"></a>
### C10 — Hilger 2016 — contrasting long-run effects of layoffs

Nathaniel G. Hilger. *Parental Job Loss and Children's Long-Term Outcomes: Evidence from 7 Million Fathers' Layoffs*. American Economic Journal: Applied Economics 8(3), July 2016, 247–283. DOI: [10.1257/app.20150295](https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20150295). [Publisher record and abstract](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fapp.20150295).

- **Population/design:** US administrative data covering roughly seven million fathers' layoffs when their children were ages 12–29; the identification strategy exploits layoff timing.
- **Measured outcome:** College enrollment and quality, early-career earnings and family income. Layoffs sharply reduced family income but produced only small average changes in the children's measured outcomes.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-to-high for the study's US setting and identification strategy.
- **Limits/access:** Publisher abstract and metadata inspected; final full text was access-restricted. Ages include adolescents and young adults, outcomes are early-adult and averages can hide subgroup differences. Different samples, shocks and controls mean C9 and C10 are contrasting estimates, not a direct replication pair.

<a id="c11"></a>
### C11 — Masten and Barnes 2018 — developmental resilience

Ann S. Masten and Andrew J. Barnes. *Resilience in Children: Developmental Perspectives*. Children 5(7), July 17, 2018, article 98. DOI: [10.3390/children5070098](https://doi.org/10.3390/children5070098). [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6069421/).

- **Population/design:** Integrative developmental review spanning varied child populations, adversities, longitudinal studies and interventions.
- **Measured outcome:** Synthesizes adaptation across developmental systems rather than one outcome; recurring systems include caring relationships, parenting, self-regulation/problem solving, routines, schools and communities.
- **Confidence:** High for defining resilience as dynamic, multisystem adaptation; strength of causal evidence varies by cited study.
- **Limits/access:** Full text inspected. Not a systematic review, and no single factor or list guarantees recovery. “Resilient” should not become a fixed child trait or a reason to withhold help.

<a id="c12"></a>
### C12 — Fritz et al. 2018 — amenable protective factors

Jessica Fritz, Anne-Marie de Graaff, Hannah Caisley, Anne-Laura van Harmelen and Paul O. Wilkinson. *A Systematic Review of Amenable Resilience Factors That Moderate and/or Mediate the Relationship Between Childhood Adversity and Mental Health in Young People*. Frontiers in Psychiatry 9, June 19, 2018, article 230. DOI: [10.3389/fpsyt.2018.00230](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2018.00230). [Full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2018.00230/full).

- **Population/design:** Systematic review of 22 cohort studies from eight countries; samples 59–6,780, mean ages 13–24. Adversity preceded or accompanied the candidate factor, which preceded psychopathology measurement.
- **Measured outcome:** Moderation/mediation of later mental-health symptoms. Support appeared for 13 of 25 individual, six of 12 family and one of five community factors studied.
- **Confidence:** Moderate for recurring associations, low for claiming that changing one factor causes recovery.
- **Limits/access:** Full text inspected. Measures and adversity definitions were heterogeneous, studies were mostly Western and observational, and socioeconomic reporting was weak. Counts reflect what was studied, not a universal ranking.

## Scarcity and stress

<a id="c13"></a>
### C13 — de Almeida et al. 2024 — financial scarcity meta-analysis

Filipa de Almeida, Ian J. Scott, Jeronimo C. Soro, Daniel Fernandes, André R. Amaral, Mafalda L. Catarino, André Arêde and Mário B. Ferreira. *Financial Scarcity and Cognitive Performance: A Meta-analysis*. Journal of Economic Psychology 101, March 2024, article 102702; online January 29, 2024. DOI: [10.1016/j.joep.2024.102702](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joep.2024.102702). [Full author PDF](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/164277/1/Financial_scarcity_and_cognitive_performance_A_meta-analysis.pdf).

- **Population/design:** Multilevel meta-analysis of 256 effects from 29 adult datasets, total N=111,852; mixed correlational, quasi-experimental and experimental designs.
- **Measured outcome:** Attention, memory, executive function and reasoning. The total estimate without education controls was *g*=-0.43; the partial estimate accounting for education was *g*=-0.15.
- **Confidence:** High for the synthesis as conducted; low for reading the pooled number as a single causal “bandwidth tax.”
- **Limits/access:** Full text inspected. Heterogeneity was substantial; multiple effects per study and bidirectional links among scarcity, education and cognition complicate causal interpretation. Children were excluded.

<a id="c14"></a>
### C14 — Mani et al. 2013 — poverty and cognitive function

Anandi Mani, Sendhil Mullainathan, Eldar Shafir and Jiaying Zhao. *Poverty Impedes Cognitive Function*. Science 341(6149), August 30, 2013, 976–980. DOI: [10.1126/science.1238041](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1238041). [Author PDF](https://shafir.scholar.princeton.edu/sites/g/files/toruqf4226/files/povertyimpedescognitivefunction.pdf).

- **Population/design:** Four studies: hypothetical financial scenarios with US mall shoppers, plus pre/post-harvest comparisons among 464 sugarcane farmers in 54 Indian villages.
- **Measured outcome:** Cognitive-control and fluid-intelligence tasks. Poorer shoppers performed worse after demanding, not easy, financial scenarios; the same farmers performed worse before harvest.
- **Confidence:** Moderate for the reported patterns, low-to-moderate for a general causal mechanism across poverty contexts.
- **Limits/access:** Full text inspected. Later critiques and replies summarized in [C16](#c16) discuss income dichotomization and ceiling effects. Harvest comparisons allow seasonal, workload and practice alternatives. These are task effects, not intelligence or character judgments.

<a id="c15"></a>
### C15 — Carvalho, Meier and Wang 2016 — before and after payday

Leandro S. Carvalho, Stephan Meier and Stephanie W. Wang. *Poverty and Economic Decision-Making: Evidence from Changes in Financial Resources at Payday*. American Economic Review 106(2), February 2016, 260–284. DOI: [10.1257/aer.20140481](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20140481). [Publisher record and full paper](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20140481).

- **Population/design:** Low-income US online-panel households randomly assigned to complete a survey before or after payday, creating real short-run variation in cash, balances and spending.
- **Measured outcome:** Monetary and real-effort intertemporal choices, risk taking, decision quality, cognitive tasks and heuristic judgments. Before-payday respondents were more present-biased only for monetary choices; the other listed outcomes showed no detectable differences.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-to-high for the study's bounded payday comparison.
- **Limits/access:** Full text inspected. Payday may not alter perceived scarcity like a hypothetical financial problem, and nulls are not proof of no effect. Different manipulation and tasks make this an alternative field test, not a failed direct replication of C14.

<a id="c16"></a>
### C16 — Szecsi and Szaszi 2024 — scarcity-cue Bayesian meta-analysis

Peter Szecsi and Barnabas Szaszi. *Financial-Scarcity-Related Cues' Impact on the Cognitive Performance of the Poor: A Meta-analysis*. Collabra: Psychology 10(1), September 24, 2024, article 122943. DOI: [10.1525/collabra.122943](https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.122943). [Full text](https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/10/1/122943/203333/Financial-Scarcity-Related-Cues-Impact-on-the).

- **Population/design:** Preregistered systematic review and Bayesian meta-analysis of 14 effects from 10 experimental/quasi-experimental scarcity-cue studies.
- **Measured outcome:** Income-by-scarcity-cue interaction on cognitive performance. Main estimate *g*=0.09, 95% credible interval [-0.03, 0.21], with moderate evidence against disproportionate impairment of lower-income participants; income-rank modeling was inconclusive.
- **Confidence:** Moderate for this narrow synthesis; conclusions changed with prior assumptions.
- **Limits/access:** Full text inspected. Few, similar, low-ecological-validity studies and limited moderator information. Successful, null and contrary findings coexist; it does not test all scarcity or the broad association in C13.

<a id="c17"></a>
### C17 — Shields, Sazma and Yonelinas 2016 — acute stress and executive function

Grant S. Shields, Matthew A. Sazma and Andrew P. Yonelinas. *The Effects of Acute Stress on Core Executive Functions: A Meta-Analysis and Comparison with Cortisol*. Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews 68, September 2016, 651–668; online June 28, 2016. DOI: [10.1016/j.neubiorev.2016.06.038](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2016.06.038). [Full author PDF](https://www.grantsshields.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/Stress_and_EF_meta-analysis.pdf).

- **Population/design:** Meta-analysis of laboratory acute-stress comparisons. For working memory alone, 34 studies, 164 effects and N=1,353; nearly all study mean ages were 18–29.
- **Measured outcome:** Small impairments in working memory (*g*=-0.197) and cognitive flexibility; no overall inhibition effect, with cognitive inhibition impaired and response inhibition improved.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-to-high for short-term laboratory effects in mostly young adults.
- **Limits/access:** Full text inspected. Results varied by task and stress features. Only two child studies fell outside the dominant young-adult age range; acute laboratory stress is not chronic adversity or poverty.

<a id="c18"></a>
### C18 — Starcke and Brand 2016 — stress and decisions under uncertainty

Katrin Starcke and Matthias Brand. *Effects of Stress on Decisions Under Uncertainty: A Meta-Analysis*. Psychological Bulletin 142(9), September 2016, 909–933; online May 23, 2016. DOI: [10.1037/bul0000060](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000060). [PubMed record and abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27213236/). Correction DOI: [10.1037/bul0000068](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000068).

- **Population/design:** Meta-analysis of 32 laboratory stress datasets, total N=1,829, comparing induced acute stress with control conditions.
- **Measured outcome:** Small average shift toward reward seeking, risk or disadvantageous choice (*d*=0.17); *d*=0.26 when risk was disadvantageous and *d*=0.01 in other task types.
- **Confidence:** Moderate for the abstract-reported average and task moderator.
- **Limits/access:** Abstract and metadata only; full methods were not accessible. Predominantly adult laboratory tasks constrain generalization. The correction concerns description of sex-specific cortisol effects. Results do not mean stress always produces bad choices.

## Children's explanations of unequal outcomes

<a id="c19"></a>
### C19 — Gonzalez, Macchia and Whillans 2022 — effort, ability and luck

Antonya Marie Gonzalez, Lucía Macchia and Ashley V. Whillans. *The Developmental Origins and Behavioral Consequences of Attributions for Inequality*. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology 101, July 2022, article 104329. DOI: [10.1016/j.jesp.2022.104329](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2022.104329). [Repository record](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/28586/), [accepted manuscript](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/28586/1/11_Gonzalez%20et%20al%20(2022).pdf).

- **Population/design:** Three preregistered US/Canadian studies, total N=698: 200 online parents; 226 parent–child dyads with children ages 7–14; and a laboratory convenience sample of children ages 7–10.
- **Measured outcome:** Choice of effort, ability or luck explanations and allocation after an explanation. Parents favored effort; parent and child attributions covaried; effort explanations increased children's perpetuation of fictional inequality relative to ability or luck.
- **Confidence:** Moderate: preregistration and experiment strengthen bounded findings, while parent–child similarity is correlational.
- **Limits/access:** Full accepted manuscript inspected. The dyad sample was affluent and mostly White; scenarios and forced explanation choices simplify causation. No universal age pattern was found within the studied range.

<a id="c20"></a>
### C20 — Leshin and Rhodes 2023 — who created unequal rules

Rachel A. Leshin and Marjorie Rhodes. *Structural Explanations for Inequality Reduce Children's Biases and Promote Rectification Only If They Implicate the High-Status Group*. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 120(35), August 29, 2023, e2310573120; online August 21. DOI: [10.1073/pnas.2310573120](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2310573120). [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10466091/).

- **Population/design:** Experiment with 206 predominantly US children ages 5.08–10.8. Children heard that a high-status group created unequal rules, a neutral third party created them, or received no structural cause.
- **Measured outcome:** Bias, judgments of hierarchy fairness and resource allocations. The high-status-creator condition reduced bias, led to lower fairness ratings and increased allocation to the low-status group.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-to-high for immediate responses in this experiment.
- **Limits/access:** Full text inspected. One fictional hierarchy, fixed outcome order and observer perspective limit transfer. The title's “reduce” is causal within this manipulation, not proof that structural explanations always work or explain an individual's misfortune.

<a id="c21"></a>
### C21 — Vasil et al. 2024 — structural explanations and rectification

Ny Vasil, Mahesh Srinivasan, Monica E. Ellwood-Lowe, Sierra Delaney, Alison Gopnik and Tania Lombrozo. *Structural Explanations Lead Young Children and Adults to Rectify Resource Inequalities*. Journal of Experimental Child Psychology 242, June 2024, article 105896. DOI: [10.1016/j.jecp.2024.105896](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2024.105896). [Full author PDF](https://cognition.princeton.edu/sites/g/files/toruqf3386/files/documents/ny2024.pdf).

- **Population/design:** Experiment with 201 children ages 3–6 (95% ages 4–5, San Francisco Bay Area) and 201 online adults. Fictional school inequality arose from stable external ingredient access, effort or no explanation.
- **Measured outcome:** Resource allocation and normative judgment. Structural explanations increased rectification; 48% of children fully rectified versus 28% giving equally. Children also generalized the inequality to a new resource.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-to-high for immediate effects in the scenario.
- **Limits/access:** Full text inspected. Convenience sample, fictional setup and no delayed or real-world outcome. Highlighting stability may clarify a structure but can also support expectations that inequality will persist; lesson wording should pair barriers with possible change.

<a id="c22"></a>
### C22 — Burkholder, Sims and Killen 2026 — structural, individual and random wealth explanations

Amanda R. Burkholder, Riley N. Sims and Melanie Killen. *How Youth Think About Structural, Individual, and Random Sources of Wealth Inequality*. Journal of Experimental Child Psychology 264, April 2026, article 106430; online December 26, 2025. DOI: [10.1016/j.jecp.2025.106430](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2025.106430). [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41455209/), [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12867206/).

- **Population/design:** Within-participant study of 262 youth ages 9–14 in the US Mid-Atlantic; racially diverse but primarily middle to high-middle income. Baseline, individual, structural and random wealth explanations were presented in fixed order.
- **Measured outcome:** Acceptability, reasoning and redistribution. Structural inequalities were judged less acceptable than individual or random ones and prompted more egalitarian responses; age groups differed in some structural beliefs about wealth.
- **Confidence:** Moderate for the sample's differentiated judgments; age differences are not causal developmental transitions.
- **Limits/access:** Full text inspected. Not preregistered, fixed condition order, hypothetical outcomes and one regional income profile. It cannot establish a universal age cutoff or cultural pattern.

## Coverage and stopping note

Searches combined DOI/publisher records, PubMed/PMC, author and institutional repositories, citation chaining and targeted searches for the five requested frameworks and empirical questions. Priority went to primary research, systematic/meta-analytic reviews, direct full text and informative conflicting or null findings. The review stopped at 22 records once each question had relevant support or an explicit evidence gap. It did not find a tested, complete ages-8+ curriculum that jointly teaches choice, circumstances, chance and recovery; C20 and C21 evaluate brief fictional explanations only.
