# Sources — habits

[Topic overview](README.md) · [All sources](../sources.md) · [Research library](../README.md)

Access date: 2026-09-06 (B1–B4) and 2026-10-09 (B5–B18). Confidence is specific to the stated finding and setting. Source identifiers stay stable across the library; an abstract-only record does not imply full methods were inspected.

### B1 — Formation time varies

Ben Singh, Andrew Murphy, Carol Maher, Ashleigh E. Smith. *Time to Form a Habit: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Health Behaviour Habit Formation and Its Determinants*. Healthcare 12(23), December 9, 2024, 2488. [Abstract and metadata](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39685110/).

Twenty studies, 2,601 participants; study mean ages 21.5–73.5, not participant age bounds. Four studies reported formation times; medians ranged 59–66 days and means 106–154, with wide individual variation. Eleven studies were rated high risk of bias. Confidence: moderate for rejecting one universal timetable, limited for causal intervention efficacy or children. PubMed abstract inspected; publisher/PMC full text was blocked. Do not present the pooled pre/post change as a controlled treatment effect.

### B2 — Recent context-consistency synthesis

Lianghao Zhu and colleagues. *Not all cues are equal: A systematic review and three-level meta-analysis of context consistency, physical activity and habit strength*. Applied Psychology: Health and Well-Being 18(4), August 2026, e70204. [Abstract and metadata](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42586550/).

Fourteen studies, 6,069 participants. Context consistency correlated with activity (r=.11) and activity-habit strength (r=.20); cue types differed. Confidence: moderate for the reported associations, not proof of causal cue interventions. Abstract inspected; full text blocked and age breakdown not verified. Do not generalize to child learning habits or prescribe one best cue from these averages.

### B3 — Within-person habit trajectories and prompts

Dario Baretta, Noemi Gillmann, Robert Edgren, Jennifer Inauen. *HabitWalk: A micro-randomized trial to understand and promote habit formation in physical activity*. Applied Psychology: Health and Well-Being 17(1), February 2025, e12605; online October 10, 2024. [Abstract and metadata](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39387277/).

Twenty-four participants, 105-day experimental phase; daily message components randomized. Habit trajectories varied and combined components showed tentative benefits. Confidence: preliminary, small sample, self-reported automaticity. Abstract inspected; PMC full text blocked. Repetition itself was not randomized. This is adult physical-activity evidence, not a validated child reminder system.

### B4 — Context and study habits

Marco Stojanovic, Axel Grund, Stefan Fries. *Context Stability in Habit Building Increases Automaticity and Goal Attainment*. Frontiers in Psychology 13, June 10, 2022, 883795. [Full article](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.883795/full).

Supports investigating consistent starting contexts for study routines. Study 1: 95 university students, mean age 23.2, six weeks, manipulated context stability. Study 2: observational app data from 218 users. Confidence: moderate and setting-dependent; self-reports, selection and incentives limit generalization. Methods, findings and limitations inspected; random assignment was not established in the inspected methods. Not a child trial or evidence that complex understanding becomes habitual.

Records B5–B18 extend the bucket to interruptions, children and family support, and changing habits. Unless stated, the abstract and publisher record were inspected, not the full text. Child evidence is thin: only B10 and B13–B18 involve children, and none tests habit formation or automaticity in primary-school children.

### B5 — A missed opportunity in habit formation

Phillippa J. Lally, Cornelia H. M. van Jaarsveld, Henry W. W. Potts, Jane Wardle. *How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world*. European Journal of Social Psychology 40(6), 2010, 998–1009 (online 2009). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674).

96 adult volunteers each chose a daily eating, drinking or activity behavior after a fixed cue (such as breakfast) for 12 weeks; 82 gave enough data; daily self-reported automaticity. "Missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not materially affect the habit formation process"; consistent performance fitted the curve better; time to plateau ranged 18–254 days. Limits: self-report, no comparison group, curve fitted for 62 people (39 well), adults, one miss at a time.

### B6 — Habit decay when trying to drop a behavior

Robert Edgren, Dario Baretta, Jennifer Inauen. *The temporal trajectories of habit decay in daily life: An intensive longitudinal study on four health-risk behaviors*. Applied Psychology: Health and Well-Being 17(1), 2025, e12612 (online 2024). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/aphw.12612).

194 adults trying to reduce sedentary time, snacking, alcohol or smoking; 91 days; daily automaticity. Decay usually slowed over time, stabilised after 1–65 days and was highly individual. Limits: non-randomised groups, self-report, health-risk behaviors, adults. Supports "weakening is gradual and varies", not a timetable.

### B7 — Habits after a change of setting

Ian Walker, Gregory O. Thomas, Bas Verplanken. *Old habits die hard: Travel habit formation and decay during an office relocation*. Environment and Behavior 47(10), 2015, 1089–1106 (online 2014). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013916514549619).

Staff of one organisation measured before and at 1 and 4 weeks after a move. Automaticity of travel weakened right after the move for everyone; for those who changed mode, the old habit decayed over weeks as the new one grew. Limits: one workplace, adults, travel, four-week follow-up. Supports "a new setting loosens cues, and change is gradual".

### B8 — Context change effects fade

Gregory O. Thomas, Wouter Poortinga, Elena Sautkina. *Habit discontinuity, self-activation, and the diminishing influence of context change: Evidence from the UK Understanding Society Survey*. PLoS ONE 11(4), 2016, e0153490. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0153490).

18,053 adult commuters in a cross-sectional survey; values guided travel mode mostly soon after a house move, and the gap narrowed over time. Full text inspected. Limits: cross-sectional, small effects (pseudo R² .06–.08), age confounding, adults. Suggests a short window after a change, not proof for children.

### B9 — A long break and achievement

Harris Cooper, Barbara A. Nye, Kelly Charlton, James J. Lindsay, Scott Greathouse. *The effects of summer vacation on achievement test scores: A narrative and meta-analytic review*. Review of Educational Research 66(3), 1996, 227–268. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543066003227).

39 studies reviewed, 13 pooled: school-age pupils scored about one tenth of a standard deviation lower after summer, more in maths than reading, with income differences. Limits: 1996, school tests rather than home practice, break and lack of instruction not separable. Shows a partial dip, not erasure.

### B10 — If-then plans in children

Jasmin Breitwieser, Tilman Reinelt. *The effectiveness of implementation intentions in children: A systematic review and meta-analysis*. British Journal of Psychology, 2026. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjop.70065).

42 studies, 12,957 children (mean age 10.7): small-to-medium benefit for goal achievement (g = 0.31), high heterogeneity, stronger in younger children. Abstract only. Limits: outcome is goal achievement, not habit or automaticity; how plans were delivered is not in the abstract.

### B11 — Plans that replace a habitual response

Marieke A. Adriaanse, Peter M. Gollwitzer, Denise T. D. De Ridder, John B. F. de Wit, Floor M. Kroese. *Breaking habits with implementation intentions: A test of underlying processes*. Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 37(4), 2011. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167211399102).

Three adult experiments: a plan naming an alternative response removed the habitual response's accessibility advantage, but did not make the alternative automatic; behavior departs from habit "only to the extent that individuals have strong alternative goal intentions". Limits: cognitive lab measures, adults, not behavior over time.

### B12 — Changing the environment

Gareth J. Hollands and colleagues. *Altering the availability or proximity of food, alcohol, and tobacco products to change their selection and consumption*. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2019(8), CD012573. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD012573.pub2).

24 randomised studies, all food, mostly lab and adult. Fewer options and greater distance reduced selection or consumption; certainty low or very low. Limits: not habits, mostly adults, snack foods in lab settings.

### B13 — Bedtime routines in young children

Jodi A. Mindell and colleagues. *Bedtime routines for young children: A dose-dependent association with sleep outcomes*. Sleep 38(5), 2015, 717–722. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.4662).

Mothers of 10,085 children aged 0–5 across 13 countries or regions: more nights with a routine went with better sleep. Limits: parent questionnaire, cross-sectional association, under school age, routine not separable from wider household structure.

### B14 — Parents and homework

Erika A. Patall, Harris M. Cooper, Jorgianne Civey Robinson. *Parent involvement in homework: A research synthesis*. Review of Educational Research 78(4), 2008, 1039–1101. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654308325185).

14 training studies: training parents raised homework completion, cut homework problems and possibly raised elementary achievement; 22 correlational samples were mixed by grade and subject; rule-setting related more strongly than other strategies. Limits: the correlational samples cannot show cause.

### B15 — Control and support in maths homework

Gintautas Šilinskas, Eve Kikas. *Parental involvement in math homework: Links to children's performance and motivation*. Scandinavian Journal of Educational Research 63(1), 2019, 17–37 (online 2017). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/00313831.2017.1324901).

512 children followed from grade 3 to 6: perceived parental support went with more task persistence; control went with worse outcomes, and low maths self-concept predicted more control. Limits: child reports and associations; the direction of cause is unclear.

### B16 — Autonomy support in homework

Eleftheria N. Gonida, Kai S. Cortina. *Parental involvement in homework: Relations with parent and student achievement-related motivational beliefs and achievement*. British Journal of Educational Psychology 84(3), 2014, 376–396. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjep.12039).

282 pupils (grades 5 and 8) and one parent each: autonomy support was the most beneficial type for motivation-related outcomes. Limits: a single survey, so associations only.

### B17 — Parental support of instrument practice

Andrea Creech. *Learning a musical instrument: the case for parental support*. Music Education Research 12(1), 2010, 13–32. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/14613800903569237).

337 parent–pupil–teacher groups in violin teaching: enjoyment, motivation and self-efficacy were higher when parents asked their child's view, negotiated practice within the teacher's limits and set up a structured practice space. Limits: survey, one instrument, association not trial.

### B18 — Family-led literacy practice

Katlynn Dahl-Leonard and colleagues. *Examining the effects of family-implemented literacy interventions for school-aged children: A meta-analysis*. Educational Psychology Review 37(1), 2025. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-025-09985-3).

22 studies: mean effect g = 0.36 (group designs) on literacy outcomes; few studies with older children. Limits: structured, taught interventions, not self-started routines; literacy outcomes, not habit.
