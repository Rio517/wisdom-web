# Sources and claim ledger

Access date: 2026-09-06. This ledger records the sources supporting the [research findings](report-source.md). Evidence descriptions are paraphrases. Confidence refers to the stated finding within its setting, not to a prediction about an individual reader.

Original papers were inspected by the research team unless an entry specifies abstract-only access or official guidance. The coordinator independently checked the central accumulation, adolescent recovery, illustrated teaching, planning, admissions, and hosting claims. No source PDFs or illustrations are reproduced in this repository.

## Lifetime opportunities

### L1 — Skill accumulation

Flavio Cunha, James J. Heckman, Susanne M. Schennach. *Estimating the Technology of Cognitive and Noncognitive Skill Formation*. Econometrica 78(3), May 2010, 883–931. [Published paper](https://hceconomics.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/pdf/events/Cunha_Heckman_etal_2010_Econometrica_v78_n3.pdf).

Supports: earlier skills can complement later investment. Structural models use longitudinal data on 2,207 firstborn white children in the Children of the NLSY/79. Findings depend on modeling assumptions and concern parental inputs and environments. The model explicitly excludes adult investment. Confidence: moderate for the mechanism; insufficient for individual forecasts or irreversible deadlines.

### L2 — Qualifications and employment

OECD. *Education at a Glance 2025*, “How does educational attainment affect participation in the labour market?” September 2025. [Official chapter](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/education-at-a-glance-2025_1c0d9c79-en/full-report/how-does-educational-attainment-affect-participation-in-the-labour-market_ae451464.html).

Supports: higher educational attainment is associated with higher employment. OECD averages for ages 25–34 in 2024 were 60% below upper secondary, 79% upper secondary/post-secondary nontertiary, and 87% tertiary. Country, skill, and field differences matter. National observational statistics do not isolate causal effects. Confidence: high descriptively; not a guarantee about any qualification or person.

### L3 — A consequential admissions threshold

Seth D. Zimmerman. *The Returns to College Admission for Academically Marginal Students*. Journal of Labor Economics 32(4), October 2014, 711–754. [Publisher record](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/676661), [published paper](https://spinup-000d1a-wp-offload-media.s3.amazonaws.com/faculty/wp-content/uploads/sites/77/2021/03/JoLE_marginal_students.pdf).

Supports: access at a real admissions threshold can affect later outcomes. A fuzzy regression-discontinuity study around Florida International University's eligibility threshold estimates attendance and earnings gains; effects vary across groups. Confidence: relatively strong causal evidence locally, conditional on design assumptions. It estimates admission effects, not the return to each extra grade or childhood study hour. Historical setting and earnings-record coverage limit generalization.

### L4 — Adolescent recovery

Jonathan Guryan, Jens Ludwig, Monica P. Bhatt and colleagues. *Not Too Late: Improving Academic Outcomes among Adolescents*. American Economic Review 113(3), March 2023, 738–765. [Published paper](https://educationlab.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2023/10/UChicago-Education-Lab-Not-Too-Late-Paper_03.23.pdf).

Supports: intensive tutoring can repair learning gaps during adolescence. Two randomized Chicago high-school trials report participant math-score effects of 0.18–0.40 standard deviations, with persistence. Participants were predominantly low-income ninth/tenth graders of colour. Confidence: high for this substantial instructional intervention; graduation estimates were imprecise. Encouragement alone is not the tested treatment.

### L5 — Adult retraining

Louis Jacobson, Robert J. LaLonde, Daniel G. Sullivan. *Estimating the Returns to Community College Schooling for Displaced Workers*. IZA Discussion Paper 1017, February 2004; published in Journal of Econometrics 125(1), 2005, 271–304. [Research record](https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/1017/estimating-the-returns-to-community-college-schooling-for-displaced-workers), [working paper](https://docs.iza.org/dp1017.pdf).

Supports: later study can improve outcomes after job displacement. Linked Washington State records cover displaced workers aged 22–60; preferred models estimate positive earnings returns, varying considerably by course type. Confidence: moderate and model-dependent. Participation is nonrandom; historical local estimates do not promise returns from every contemporary course.

### L6 — Adult learning constraints

OECD. *Trends in Adult Learning: New Data from the 2023 Survey of Adult Skills*, “Why is participation not more common?” June 2025. [Official chapter](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/trends-in-adult-learning_ec0624a6-en/full-report/why-is-participation-not-more-common_73893b53.html).

Supports: work/family responsibilities and cost can obstruct desired training. Among adults with unmet training desires, 48% named time constraints and 13% cost as their main barrier; these are unweighted participating-country averages for ages 25–65. Confidence: high for the survey description. The denominator is not all adults, and this is not evidence of a steady decline with age.

### L7 — Mindset review emphasizing study quality

Brooke N. Macnamara, Alexander P. Burgoyne. *Do growth mindset interventions impact students’ academic achievement? A systematic review and meta-analysis with recommendations for best practices*. Psychological Bulletin 149(3–4), 2023; online November 3, 2022. [Record and abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36326645/).

Supports: large universal achievement claims are unjustified. Across 63 studies the overall effect was small; the authors' highest-quality subset estimated d=.02, with a confidence interval including zero. Confidence: strong caution, conditional on review methods and quality coding. Full paper inspected by the lifetime researcher; coordinator's PubMed retrieval returned no article text. Read alongside L8.

### L8 — Mindset review emphasizing context

Jeni L. Burnette and colleagues. *A systematic review and meta-analysis of growth mindset interventions: For whom, how, and why might such interventions work?* Psychological Bulletin 149(3–4), 2023; online October 13, 2022. [Record and abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36227318/).

Supports: small benefits may depend on population and implementation. Review of 53 independent randomized samples; targeted populations with high implementation fidelity yielded an achievement estimate of d=.14. The prediction interval included zero. Abstract and metadata inspected, not full methods. Confidence: moderate for the reported conditional result. Different review methods prevent a simple numerical reconciliation with L7.

### L9 — Admissions are not guaranteed by grades

Harvard College. *Frequently Asked Questions*, admissions criteria and age eligibility entries. Undated, current page accessed 2026-09-06. [Official FAQ](https://college.harvard.edu/resources/faq).

Supports: Harvard considers academic accomplishment alongside other criteria and states no admission formula; it does not impose a general applicant age requirement. Confidence: high for the institution's stated policy. This does not establish the admissions rules of other institutions or a person's admission probability.

### L10 — Adult educational support exists

U.S. Department of Education, Federal Student Aid. *Don’t Miss Out on Federal Pell Grants*. Undated, current page includes 2026–27 information. [Official article](https://studentaid.gov/articles/dont-miss-out-on-pell-grants/).

Supports: eligible undergraduates may receive Pell Grants regardless of age; prior study need not have been consecutive. Confidence: high for current stated eligibility. Other eligibility requirements apply. This is a counterexample to a blanket age-exclusion claim, not an assessment of anyone's aid eligibility or proof of equal access at every age.

## Teaching and development

### T1 — Age-responsive practice

Harvard Center on the Developing Child. *Enhancing and Practicing Executive Function Skills with Children from Infancy to Adolescence*. May 6, 2014. [Resource page](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/handouts-tools/activities-guide-enhancing-and-practicing-executive-function-skills/).

Supports: official guidance recommends play involving planning, rules, monitoring, and adapting strategy, including sections for ages 5–7 and 7–12. Adult support and challenge should adjust with skill. Confidence: high as an account of guidance; individual activities and lifetime transfer are not established by this guide. Full guide inspected by the teaching researcher.

### T2 — Elementary decision concepts

Alliance for Decision Education. *Decision Education K–12 Learning Standards*, first edition, 2023. [Standards PDF](https://alliancefordecisioneducation.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/DE-Learning-Standards-Landscape-V5-WEB.pdf).

Supports: elementary teaching can be organized around alternatives, affected people, uncertainty, reasons, habits, and the distinction between decision process and outcome. Includes considering whether to continue or abandon a project. Confidence: high as a curriculum reference. These are original standards, not an effectiveness trial or proof of developmental readiness for every child.

### T3 — Model, practice, reflect

Education Endowment Foundation. *Metacognition and Self-Regulated Learning*, second edition, November 13, 2025; related toolkit synthesis updated May 2025. [Guidance](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/metacognition), [evidence synthesis](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/metacognition-and-self-regulation).

Supports: explicit planning, monitoring, evaluation, modeled reasoning, guided practice, and fading support within concrete subject tasks. Confidence: high for the guidance and positive attainment evidence overall. Evaluation independence varies; reported average effects are not an estimate for this manual. Official research synthesis, not an original experiment.

### T4 — Illustrated reasoning instruction

Allen Nsangi and colleagues. *Informed Health Choices primary-school intervention: one-year follow-up* (abbreviated title). Trials 21, article 27, January 6, 2020. [Full article and title](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13063-019-3960-9).

Supports: specific reasoning gains from a teacher-supported package in 120 Ugandan schools. One-year adjusted test-score difference: 16.7 percentage points, 95% CI 13.9–19.5. Confidence: high within this setting; general transfer and comics alone were not tested. Attrition differed; assessment was researcher-developed.

### T5 — Obstacles and action plans

Angela Lee Duckworth, Teri A. Kirby, Anton Gollwitzer, Gabriele Oettingen. *From Fantasy to Action: Mental Contrasting With Implementation Intentions Improves Academic Performance in Children*. Social Psychological and Personality Science, first online February 11, 2013. [Full paper](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/wp.nyu.edu/dist/c/6235/files/2019/02/duckworth-et-al-2013-from-fantasy-to-action.pdf).

Supports: planning around a realistic obstacle can help near-term goal pursuit. Randomized trial of 77 fifth graders, mean age 11.05, at one urban school; three training hours produced third-quarter improvements relative to positive thinking. Fourth-quarter differences diminished and were nonsignificant. Confidence: moderate; durable habit change and effects for ages 7–9 remain unestablished.

### T6 — Near-future rehearsal

A. Kretschmer-Trendowicz, K. M. Schnitzspahn, L. Reuter, M. Altgassen. *Episodic future thinking improves children’s prospective memory performance in a complex task setting with real life task demands*. Psychological Research, online August 31, 2017; issue April 2019. [Record and abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28861602/).

Supports: future imagery improved remembering intended actions in a laboratory task. Randomized experiment with 56 children, mean age 10.73. Abstract and metadata inspected; full methods not inspected. Confidence: moderate for the reported task-specific effect. This does not test wise choices, adult-future connectedness, or lasting general planning ability.

### T7 — Limits to future-imagery transfer

Annick F. N. Tanguay and colleagues. *Using an episodic specificity induction to improve children’s future thinking*. Frontiers in Psychology, October 19, 2023. [Full article](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1249090/full).

Supports: richer imagined scenes need not improve other future-oriented behaviors. Randomized online study of 66 children aged 6–7; evidence for later descriptive improvements was weak, with no improvement in delay choices, prospective memory, or future-item selection. Confidence: moderate for this small study. Differences from T6 in age, task, and intervention prevent a direct contradiction.

### T8 — No marshmallow destiny

Jessica F. Sperber, Deborah Lowe Vandell, Greg J. Duncan, Tyler W. Watts. *Delay of gratification and adult outcomes: The Marshmallow Test does not reliably predict adult functioning*. Child Development, online July 29, 2024. [Record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39073534/), [full article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11581930/).

Supports: a preschool waiting task is not a reliable label for adult potential. Preregistered observational follow-up of 702 participants to age 26 found little robust prediction after adjustment. Confidence: high for caution about this task; not evidence that all self-regulation is irrelevant. Sample and task ceiling limit inference. Full article inspected by the teaching researcher; coordinator retrieval encountered a browser challenge.

### T9 — Developmental context

Harvard Center on the Developing Child. *InBrief: Executive Function*. May 20, 2012. [Official summary](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/inbriefs/inbrief-executive-function/).

Supports: working memory, mental flexibility, and self-control develop over childhood and beyond, with a role for experience. Confidence: high as a broad developmental synthesis; not evidence of a universal maturity cutoff or individualized age-based prediction. Official page inspected by the coordinator. Use for optional explanatory context, not an age-scoring algorithm.

## Search coverage and stopping rule

Search families covered Cunha/Heckman skill formation; OECD attainment and adult training; Zimmerman admissions thresholds; adolescent tutoring; displaced-worker retraining; conflicting mindset reviews; Harvard executive-function guidance; elementary decision standards; EEF metacognition; Informed Health Choices trials; child implementation intentions; future-thinking experiments; marshmallow follow-ups; and official GitHub/Astro publishing documentation.

Targeted follow-ups sought original papers, later remediation, uncertain or null effects, exact populations and denominators, current adult-aid eligibility, and limits on transfer. General intelligence, compulsory-schooling, and working-memory sources were considered during discovery but not retained where closer evidence served the question.

The focused search stops because each consequential claim has support or an explicit limit, the central disagreements are bounded, and further broad discovery is unlikely to change the proposed chapter. Reader comprehension and response are the next material unknowns. This is not a systematic review; no claim of exhaustive literature coverage is made.
