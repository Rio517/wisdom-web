## Story

### Chapter · The pair

#### 01 pair · Rider and elephant.

Picture a small rider on a big elephant. The rider plans ahead. The strong, quick elephant carries feelings and does what it has learned.

Jonathan Haidt, a psychologist, came up with this picture. It helps us think about choices. It doesn’t show how a real brain works.

Picture: the hero. A big, calm elephant stands on a wide path that runs on toward low hills. A small rider sits on its neck, one hand resting on its head, looking ahead. No reins. Each name is a plain label close to its partner (Rider above the rider, Elephant beside the elephant's body), with no line between label and figure.

- rider: Rider
- elephant: Elephant

### Chapter · Two strengths

#### 02 quick · Strong and quick.

Maya doesn’t think about her feet anymore. Practice trained her elephant, and now it does the dribbling.

The elephant pulls your hand back from a hot pan before you think. It can feel that something is off before you know why.

Picture: Lesson 2’s last picture, picked up again. Maya dribbles with her eyes up, and her elephant walks right behind her. Two small moments wait further along the same ground: a pan on a stove, and a wobbly plank over a stream.

- feet: Trained feet
- pan: Hot pan
- off: Uneasy feeling

#### 03 slow · Slow and careful.

The rider is good at things that take time. It plans and imagines what might happen.

Alfredo’s rider pictured the rest of the hike with too little water. So Alfredo and his dad turned back. Next time, his rider planned water for there and back.

Picture: the trail map from Lesson 1’s hike, with Alfredo and his dad at the trailhead. A dotted what-if runs on to the lake and ends at an empty bottle. Their backpack card is beside them.

- whatif: Rest of the trail
- back: Way back
- card: Water both ways

#### 03b now · Now or later.

The rider can also notice what matters right now.

Everyone is waiting in the car, and your jacket zip is stuck. Your elephant wants to fight it until it works. Your rider sees that it can wait. You get in the car and fix it there.

Picture: a family car at the kerb with its back door open and the family inside, ready to go. The elephant and its rider walk up to the open door. On the ground beside them lies a jacket with its zip stuck halfway. Nobody in the car is cross.

- zip: Stuck zip
- car: Everyone waiting

### Chapter · Steering

#### 04 reins · Pulling the reins.

At the park, a friend keeps changing the rules. Your elephant wants to yell. Your rider pulls the reins, and you bite your tongue.

That works for a moment. But the elephant is much bigger than the rider, and soon it pulls again.

Picture: a park with a chalk game on the path. The friend stands beyond it with the ball, and a new line in fresh yellow chalk runs across the old game. The friend is having fun, not being mean. The elephant stands close to the game, and the rider, small on its neck, holds the reins.

- rules: New rule

#### 05 path · Easier paths.

People with good self-control tend not to fight their elephant much. They train it, and they give it easier paths.

You step away for a breath, then say what isn’t fair. Or you all agree on the rules first.

Picture: the same park. A short path leads a few steps away from the chalk game to a tree, where the elephant stands, calm, and the rider’s hand rests on its head again. Back at the game, the friend waits with the ball. The two labels sit along the easy path, one near the tree and one near the game.

- breath: A breath first
- agree: Rules agreed

### Chapter · One team

#### 06 team · One team.

Neither one is the real you. The rider isn’t the boss, and the elephant isn’t the problem.

“We are the rider, and we are the elephant,” writes Jonathan Haidt.

They travel best together.

Picture: the elephant and its rider walking together up a gentle rise toward a lookout, the path behind them.

#### 07 first · Reasons afterwards.

Sometimes the elephant decides first. Then the rider finds a reason and believes it.

A new food looks odd, and you push it away. When someone asks why, your rider says you aren’t hungry.

Picture: the elephant has already turned its back on a plate. Up on its neck, the rider gives a reason, written as a plain label just above the rider.

- kicker: For older readers · optional
- reason: Not hungry

### Chapter · Takeaways

#### 08 keep · Three ideas to keep.

- **Two parts, one team.** You are the rider and the elephant, working together.
- **The elephant learns.** It learns from what you do again and again.
- **Training and easy paths.** Training the elephant and choosing easy paths tend to work better than pulling the reins.

Picture: the elephant and its rider on the open path again, small in a wide landscape.

#### 09 next · Coming next.

The elephant loves paths it already knows. Those paths are habits, and they come next.

Picture: the elephant walks along a well-worn trail that it has walked many times.

## Activity

One activity, drawn in three frames: the start, the middle of the sort, and the end. The chapter trail keeps its Activity stop between One team and Takeaways.

The mechanism is Lesson 1’s drag-to-sort board (the hike’s packing sort): a pile of cards in the middle, one side for each partner, drag a card to a side or tap a card and then a side. The elephant and its rider stand above the board, the elephant over its side and the rider over the other, each named with a plain label. There is no score, no count and no right-or-wrong mark. When a card lands, its quiet line shows under the story text. A card dropped on the other partner’s side slides gently across to its own, and the same quiet line shows. At the end both sides are full, and each gets its strength.
