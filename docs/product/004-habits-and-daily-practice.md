# 004 — Habits and the choices we repeat

Status: Proposed explanation; separate lesson and next priority agreed. Created: 2026-09-06. Updated: 2026-09-14.

## Purpose and placement

Explain how someone can make a useful action easier to return to, and how to change a routine that no longer helps. Habits deserve a dedicated, linked explanation for ages 8+, rather than only a footnote in the lifetime chapter.

Placement: its own lesson immediately after Choices–Paths and before “How we make choices,” as recorded in the [lesson roadmap](../README.md#lesson-roadmap). Habits has higher near-term priority than decision psychology for the intended readers. A small, complete lesson is worthwhile; it does not need to become a long chapter. The current lifetime lesson may include a short bridge, but that does not replace this separate explanation. Do not add an empty navigation destination now.

The [habits research bucket](../research/habits/README.md) is the source of truth for findings, citations, and unfinished research. This proposal describes an educational experience, not a tested behavior-change program.

## Core distinction

Choosing a goal, remembering to begin, practicing effectively, and understanding something difficult are different tasks. A routine can help with starting; it does not remove the need for thinking, feedback, or rest.

Proposed child-facing framing:

> You can make a choice now that helps you begin again later. Decide what you want to practice, what will remind you, and what you need ready. Then notice whether your plan helps—and change it when it does not.

Introduce **habit** through a concrete cue and response, then explain **automaticity** as needing less deliberate prompting. Do not equate every repeated action with a strong habit, or label a child as disciplined or undisciplined based on ease of starting.

## First-chapter bridge

The opening revision in [008 — Choices and consequences](008-choices-and-consequences.md) introduces repeated choices through the same fictional learner, Mika. Offer “See how it adds up” and “What makes the next start easier?” within the worked homework example. Show repeated opportunities to understand, seek feedback and return, while naming circumstances Mika does not control. A manageable starting example is opening the notebook with a pencil ready after a familiar daily activity, with adult help where needed. This is a proposed educational adaptation, not a validated child routine.

Keep this to one small layered explanation with a return to the same scene. Distinguish accumulated understanding, practical preparation and cue-linked starting; none establishes a general habit of making good decisions in every domain. No tracking system, habit score, deadline, or promise that the routine will become automatic. The current chapter must make sense without a future page existing.

## Proposed guided explanation

1. **Wanting and beginning:** compare a vague intention with a specific action in the same fictional situation.
2. **Notice the cue:** identify what happens just before the action. Distinguish a starting cue from the reason the goal matters.
3. **Make the start workable:** choose a manageable first action, prepare materials, and include help when the setting is not under the child's control.
4. **Repeat and learn:** show several attempts with feedback and variation. Making the start familiar is different from mastering the whole activity.
5. **Change what is not helping:** inspect an unhelpful cue/action pattern, choose an alternative response, and adjust the setting with adult support. Do not prescribe clinical treatment or imply that replacing any habit is simple.
6. **Restart and review:** show an interruption, a reasonable restart, and a decision about whether the routine still serves the goal. Changing or ending a routine can be a good choice.

Use the existing guided/read-mode structure, optional examples, and precise map/callout animation. This sequence is a proposal pending the child-focused evidence work below.

## A small practical experiment

Let a reader construct a plan for something they choose: reading, a musical passage, a puzzle, or preparing for a sports practice. The activity is optional and can be discussed or written on paper; no account or data submission.

- What do I want to return to, and why?
- What specific moment will remind me to start?
- What is one manageable action, and what needs to be ready?
- What might get in the way, and who could help?
- When will I check whether the plan is useful?
- After an interruption, what is my next reasonable opportunity to try?

A review date is an opportunity to learn about the plan, not a deadline for forming a habit. Do not use streak loss, leaderboards, public comparisons, or shame as motivation. Rest and freely chosen play remain worthwhile, not failures to optimize.

## Evidence and boundaries

The current research includes a 2024 formation-time review, a 2025-issue micro-randomized walking study, an August 2026 context-consistency meta-analysis, and a learning-specific 2022 study. Findings and access limits are kept in the [habits findings](../research/habits/README.md) and [source ledger](../research/habits/sources.md).

The evidence does not justify a universal number of days, effortless mastery, or calling this proposed child activity validated. Keep building a new habit separate from changing an established one. A cue diagram explains a mechanism; it is not a claim that all behavior follows one universal loop.

Before drafting the full chapter, extend the research on:

- Habit formation and routine support in primary-school children and families.
- Changing existing habits, replacing responses, and changes of context.
- Interruptions and restarting, including what evidence supports specific recovery advice.
- Autonomy, enjoyment, rewards, environmental constraints, and differing support needs.

Refresh the search near authoring time and record methods, populations, outcomes, and contrary findings. Do not postpone the lifetime chapter while these future-section questions remain open.

## Acceptance criteria

- A reader can distinguish the goal, cue, action, and support in a new example.
- The explanation distinguishes starting practice from learning well.
- A reader can revise a plan after a difficulty without interpreting it as a permanent personal defect.
- A reader understands that familiarity does not mean a routine is still useful.
- Every child-facing recommendation has either relevant evidence or a clear educational-adaptation label in contributor notes.
- Guided, continuous-reading, keyboard, touch, and reduced-motion versions preserve the same ideas.
