Lesson 1, [*Choices — The paths we make*](../the-paths-we-make/), is a guided, illustrated journey about how choices add up over a life, and about how much of what happens is not up to the person living it.

It opens with a wide map of many possible paths, then narrows into two concrete stories: a hike that runs into trouble twice, once by planning and once by weather, and a girl named Maya who tries the same start on two imagined paths. A short game lets the reader spend ten fictional afternoons of their own, choosing between soccer, cello and rest, and a free explorer lets them build an example life from age three to seventy. It closes with three takeaways.

These notes are the adult companion to that lesson, and to the [lesson overview](../) it sits alongside. Reading through with the games takes about 15 minutes; the [text-only version](../the-paths-we-make/read/) reads in about five. It's written to work from about age eight, and it works best read together, with room to stop and talk.

## What it teaches

The lesson makes three claims, and keeps each one modest on purpose:

- **Choices add up.** Small things done again and again (practising, showing up, asking for help) can grow into something larger, for better or worse. One hard day doesn't decide the future.
- **Learning builds on learning.** What someone already knows or can do gives the next thing a place to start, sometimes even in a related activity.
- **Not everything is yours to choose.** Other people's decisions, the support available, and plain chance all shape which paths stay open.

It leaves some things out on purpose, too. It does not explain how people make decisions; that's a later lesson, "How we make choices." It is not career advice, and none of the fictional paths are ranked against one another: a life spent coaching, caring for family or working a trade is not shown as smaller than one spent in a lab or a boardroom. Academic performance is treated as one kind of progress among several, not a measure of a child's worth.

## How to use it together

Before starting, it helps to say plainly that the people and choices are made up, and that the pictures aren't a measurement of anyone's real chances, just a way of showing an idea.

During the lesson, let the reader set the pace. Next always continues the story even if a choice is skipped, so there's no wrong way through.

Afterwards, a few questions tied to specific moments tend to work better than a general "so what did you learn":

- At the halfway water bottle on the hike: what would you have done, and why?
- After the drag-to-sort board: which card was hardest to place under "their choice" or "outside their control"?
- After Maya's basketball season: what did Path A Maya already know how to do that helped her in a new sport? Has anything like that happened for you?
- After your own ten afternoons: looking back, what would you do differently next time?
- In the explorer: which gray line, a path you didn't take, would you want to go back and try?
- At the end: which of the three takeaways feels most true to something in your own life right now?

Two or three questions are plenty for one sitting.

## What the evidence says, and doesn't

Earlier learning can genuinely support later learning. Relevant prior knowledge can help someone make sense of something new, though the wrong prior idea can also mislead ([How People Learn II, 2018](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/24783/chapter/7)). A related skill can give a head start, but the evidence for that is thinner than a tidy example can make it look. A systematic review of transfer between sports found mostly short-term, lower-quality studies, and only two that looked at later learning at all ([Oppici and Panchuk, 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1469029221002363)). Treat those two scenes as illustrations of a possible, partial head start that still needs adapting, not a promise that any two activities will connect this way.

Circumstances, support and chance are not decoration here; they are one of the lesson's three takeaways, and the research agrees they matter. An outside event can change what happens next, sometimes a great deal and sometimes only a little, and recovery afterwards is a process supported by relationships, schools and services, not willpower alone (see the [circumstances research](https://github.com/Rio517/wisdom-web/blob/main/docs/research/circumstances/README.md)). That's also why the lesson keeps returning to asking for help and changing plans, as Alfredo and his dad do when they reach the closed trail.

None of this means a particular child's future is decided by age eight, or by anything in this lesson.

## What is illustration, not data

A few things in the lesson look precise but aren't. The path map's height and shape mean nothing; there's no measurement behind how large a branch looks. Maya's skill bars and the ten-afternoons game use a made-up model of practice with no real numbers behind it; the point is the pattern (early progress feels slow, then some skills build on others), not the specific bars. The explorer's example choices at each age are examples of the kinds of choices people make, not a ranking of good and bad lives and not a prediction about the reader. Every person in the lesson is fictional.

## Accessibility and reading options

The whole journey works with the keyboard alone: the ← and → keys move forward and back. It respects a reader's reduced-motion setting by jumping straight to each final picture instead of animating. A full text version covers the same content for anyone who would rather read than click through. It's built for desktop and iPad-sized screens; phone layout is planned but not ready yet.

## One more thing

This is early material, reviewed by its authors more than tested with readers. If something here misleads, confuses, or gets a fact wrong, the [source repository](https://github.com/Rio517/wisdom-web) is open, and notes are welcome.
